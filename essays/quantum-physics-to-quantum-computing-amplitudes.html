Quantum Physics to Quantum Computing

A photodetector clicks like a very ordinary device. At the end of an experiment, it reports one classical fact: the photon arrived here. The strange part happened earlier. Before the click, the calculation did not keep a list of ordinary chances. It kept amplitudes, numbers that can point in different directions in the complex plane and add like vectors.

That is the first habit to build for quantum computing. A qubit is described by a state vector

$$ |\psi\rangle=\alpha |0\rangle+\beta |1\rangle. $$

The symbols $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are basis states, like coordinate axes. The complex numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are amplitudes along those axes. Measurement turns them into probabilities through the Born rule:

$$ P(0)=|\alpha|^2,\qquad P(1)=|\beta|^2. $$

Normalization says all the eventual probabilities must add to one:

$$ |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1. $$

This is already different from a hidden coin. A coin with a 50 percent chance of heads has a probability table. A qubit with equal probabilities has many possible states, because the relative phase between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can change future interference.

Think of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as arrows drawn in their own complex planes. Their lengths set immediate measurement probabilities. Their directions set how later additions will behave. Multiplying the whole state by the same complex phase rotates both arrows together and changes no experiment. Rotating only one arrow relative to the other changes future experiments. Quantum mechanics cares about relative phase.

That distinction explains a common beginner mistake. The state

$$ \frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} $$

and the state

$$ e^{i\gamma}\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} $$

describe the same physical qubit for any global phase $\gamma$. The state

$$ \frac{|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} $$

changes as $\phi$ changes, because the two basis directions now have a different relationship to one another.

The square in $|\alpha|^2$ is a length squared. If $\alpha$ is a complex number, $|\alpha|$ means its distance from the origin in the complex plane. The phase, the direction of that complex number, disappears from this single probability. It has not become useless. It is waiting for a later gate to compare it against another phase.

That waiting phase is the reason quantum algorithms feel slippery at first. If a circuit prepares

$$ \frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}, $$

then measuring immediately gives $0$ and $1$ equally often. If a circuit prepares

$$ \frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}, $$

the immediate measurement probabilities are still equal. A computational-basis measurement cannot see the sign. A Hadamard gate can. It converts those two equal-looking states into different basis states:

$$ H\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|0\rangle,\qquad H\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|1\rangle. $$

So phase is information. It is information that becomes visible only after the right transformation.

A useful mental rule is to keep probability out of the story until measurement arrives. Before that moment, the circuit is moving a vector through a complex space. Gates rotate, reflect, and phase-shift that vector. Branches of a calculation meet and add. Some additions make long arrows, some make short arrows, and some cancel nearly to zero.

That is the bridge from quantum physics to quantum computing. The physics gives the rule for amplitudes. Computing asks how to arrange those amplitudes so that the final measurement is biased toward a useful classical answer. The click at the end is ordinary. The preparation that made that click likely is where the quantum work happened.
