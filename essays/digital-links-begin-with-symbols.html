Digital Links Begin With Symbols

Here is a question that might seem obvious until you actually think about it: when you send a text message, what physically travels between your phone and the cell tower?

Not letters. Not numbers. Not bits. The channel carries a continuously varying physical quantity: an electromagnetic wave, a voltage on a wire, a beam of light. Yet somewhere at the far end, the receiver produces your exact string of characters, not approximately, not mostly, but exactly. How does a physical wave carry information so precisely?

The answer starts with a surprisingly simple idea: symbols. And understanding symbols thoroughly will unlock the entire architecture of modern digital communication.

The gap between bits and waves

Imagine you want to transmit the letter "A". In ASCII, that is the byte 01000001, eight bits. You need to send those eight bits across some physical medium. The medium does not have an "on" and "off" switch. It has a voltage, a frequency, an amplitude, a phase. These are continuous quantities.

You could try to map "bit 0 is low voltage, bit 1 is high voltage" and transmit one bit at a time. That works, and early modems did roughly this. But it turns out to be wasteful in a precise mathematical sense that we will explore.

The better approach is to transmit several bits at once using a symbol.

A symbol is one choice from a finite alphabet. Instead of saying "this moment carries one bit", you say "this moment carries one of M possible values, which represents log2(M) bits." With M=4, each symbol carries 2 bits. With M=16, each symbol carries 4 bits. With M=256, each symbol carries 8 bits.

The key insight: you are not transmitting the bits themselves. You are transmitting a physical measurement that encodes them. The receiver measures that physical quantity and maps back to bits.

Building the simplest alphabet: 2-PAM

Start with the simplest case. Two symbols, two voltages. Symbol "0" is transmitted as voltage -1. Symbol "1" is transmitted as voltage +1. The receiver measures the voltage and thresholds: positive means "1", negative means "0". This is binary PAM, or 2-PAM.

Now extend it. Instead of two voltages, use four: -3, -1, +1, +3. Now one measurement carries two bits. You assign:

  • voltage -3 means "00"
  • voltage -1 means "01"
  • voltage +1 means "11"
  • voltage +3 means "10"

Notice the assignment is not arbitrary. Adjacent voltage levels are assigned codes that differ by only one bit. This is Gray coding, and it is clever. When noise pushes a symbol toward a neighboring level (the most common error), only one bit flips instead of multiple. Your bit error rate stays low even when symbol errors happen occasionally.

Notice what happened between the two slides. We went from 2 symbols to 4, from 1 bit per symbol to 2 bits per symbol. The symbol rate (how many symbols per second) stayed the same, but the bit rate doubled. This is one of the fundamental levers in communication system design.

Moving into two dimensions: QAM

PAM only uses one physical dimension: amplitude. But when you transmit over a carrier (a radio wave at some frequency), you gain access to a second dimension for free.

A carrier wave at frequency $f_c$ looks like $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$. You can modulate the amplitude of this cosine. But there is another sinusoid at the same frequency that is perpendicular to this one: $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$. It is called the quadrature component.

These two sinusoids are orthogonal. If you transmit a signal on the cosine and a signal on the sine, they do not interfere with each other. The receiver can separate them perfectly. So you get two real dimensions to work with.

This gives you Quadrature Amplitude Modulation, or QAM. You choose a point in a two-dimensional plane:

$$ s(t) = I \cdot \cos(2\pi f_c t) - Q \cdot \sin(2\pi f_c t) $$

The point $(I, Q)$ is your symbol. The constellation is the set of all possible $(I, Q)$ points.

QPSK uses four points, one in each quadrant, each point encoding 2 bits. 16-QAM uses 16 points arranged in a 4x4 grid, each encoding 4 bits. 64-QAM uses 64 points, each encoding 6 bits. 256-QAM gives 8 bits per symbol.

Sixteen points where there were four. Four bits per symbol where there were two. Same bandwidth, doubled bit rate. This is the magic of denser constellations.

The price of packing more points

But wait. If packing more symbols into a constellation always increases throughput, why not use 1024-QAM all the time?

Because of noise.

When you pack more points into the same power budget, the points get closer together. And noise is always present. It pushes the received point away from where the transmitter intended. If the noise pushes it far enough to cross into the territory of a neighboring point, a symbol error happens.

Think about the distance between nearest-neighbor constellation points. In 4-QAM with a given average power, the minimum distance between points is relatively large. In 16-QAM with the same average power, there are more points crowded into the same area, so the minimum distance shrinks. In 256-QAM, it shrinks further still.

The question "which constellation should I use" is really the question "how much noise is in my channel?" A deep fade on a wireless channel might force you down to QPSK to maintain reliable communication. A clean, high-power wired channel might support 1024-QAM comfortably.

The noise clouds that were safely separated in 4-QAM now overlap heavily in 16-QAM. The receiver cannot tell which point was sent. This is a concrete visualization of why higher-order constellations demand cleaner channels.

Bits per symbol: the formula and its intuition

For M symbols, the number of bits per symbol is:

$$ k = \log_2 M $$

This formula has an elegant meaning. You have M equally likely choices to communicate. The information content of one such choice is $\log_2 M$ bits. Shannon's information theory tells us this is the right measure of uncertainty.

Some concrete numbers worth knowing: 2-PAM carries 1 bit. 4-QAM (= QPSK) carries 2 bits. 8-PSK carries 3 bits. 16-QAM carries 4 bits. 64-QAM carries 6 bits. 256-QAM carries 8 bits. 1024-QAM, used in Wi-Fi 6 and 5G, carries 10 bits per symbol.

Modern 5G can dynamically switch between these based on measured channel quality. When you are close to a tower, your phone might use 256-QAM. When you are at the edge of coverage, it falls back to QPSK. The system adapts to channel conditions in real time.

From symbol to waveform

A symbol is still just a number. The transmitter needs to convert it into something that can actually be transmitted across the channel. This requires pulse shaping.

The transmitter generates a pulse at each symbol time and scales that pulse by the symbol value. If the pulse is $p(t)$ and the symbol sequence is $\{a_k\}$, then:

$$ x(t) = \sum_k a_k \, p(t - kT) $$

Here $T$ is the symbol period, and $1/T$ is the symbol rate (called the baud rate). The pulses from different symbol times overlap in time to form a continuous waveform.

The choice of $p(t)$ matters enormously. A rectangular pulse is easy to generate but creates problems in frequency (it has very wide bandwidth). A carefully designed pulse can concentrate energy in a small bandwidth while avoiding interference between neighboring symbols.

This is the topic of the next essay in this series. For now, picture the transmitted waveform as a train of pulses, each one carrying one symbol's worth of information.

The system view

Let us step back and look at what the full digital communication chain does:

Source bits arrive at the transmitter. The transmitter groups them into chunks (log2(M) bits each) and maps each chunk to a symbol in the constellation. The symbol sequence is then converted to a continuous-time waveform by pulse shaping. The waveform is moved to a carrier frequency for transmission. The channel adds noise and distortion. The receiver moves the signal back to baseband, filters it, samples it, and maps each sample back to a symbol decision. Finally, the symbols are converted back to bits.

Each step introduces potential failure modes. Noise corrupts samples. Timing errors cause samples to land in the wrong place. Carrier frequency errors rotate the constellation. Multiple users interfere with each other. The remaining essays in this series address each of these problems.

The Shannon limit: how much is possible?

Before diving into the engineering details, it is worth knowing where the fundamental ceiling is. Claude Shannon proved in 1948 that no matter how clever your modulation, the maximum rate at which you can reliably communicate through a noisy channel is:

$$ C = B \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right) $$

where $B$ is the bandwidth (in Hz), $S$ is the signal power, and $N$ is the noise power in that bandwidth.

This is the Shannon capacity. It tells you that more bandwidth and more signal-to-noise ratio give you more capacity. Every real system operates below this limit, some closer than others. Modern coding schemes like turbo codes and LDPC codes can get within a fraction of a decibel of the Shannon limit.

The formula also tells you something deep: if you could somehow have zero noise ($N = 0$), the capacity would be infinite. You could pack infinitely many bits into a single symbol by using infinitely many constellation points. In practice, noise is always present, and the minimum distance between constellation points sets a floor on the symbol error rate.

Understanding where the Shannon limit comes from, and why it is achievable, is one of the most beautiful stories in all of applied mathematics. But that is a different series. For this series, the limit gives us a useful benchmark: a well-designed system should be able to get reasonably close to it.

Every piece of every modern wireless system uses these ideas. Wi-Fi, LTE, 5G, satellite, cable modem, digital TV: all of them are built on the same conceptual foundation that starts with this simple question: how do you turn a sequence of bits into a physical wave and back again?

The answer is symbols. And symbols, it turns out, have remarkable depth.
