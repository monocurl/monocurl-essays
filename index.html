Algebra

  1. Why is a negative times a negative positive?Multiplying by a negative number flips the number line about zero, and two flips cancel. We see it, then prove it from the distributive law.
  2. The distributive law as a picture: FOIL and (a+b)²Cutting a rectangle into pieces explains FOIL, why (a+b)² has a middle term 2ab, and why (a+b)(a−b) = a² − b². Each identity is then proved.
  3. Completing the square and the quadratic formulaSolve x² + 6x = 16 by rearranging a square and a rectangle into a bigger square with one corner missing, then turn that picture into the quadratic formula.
  4. What slope means, and why y = mx + b is a lineA phone bill that grows by the same amount per gigabyte plots as a straight line. Similar triangles explain why, and give slope and y = mx + b their meaning.
  5. Solving two equations at once: where lines meetTen heads and 28 legs: how many chickens and rabbits? Each clue is a line of possibilities, and elimination pivots a line about the point where they cross.
  6. Why x⁰ = 1, and negative and fractional exponentsPowers of 2 halve as the exponent drops, and one rule, add exponents when you multiply, forces 2⁰ = 1, 2⁻¹ = 1/2 and 2^(1/2) = √2.
  7. Absolute value is distance: solving |x − a| < rRead |x − 3| < 2 as "x is within 2 of 3" and the answer, 1 < x < 5, can be seen on a number line. Also why multiplying by a negative flips an inequality.

Geometry

  1. Why the angles of a triangle add to 180°Tear the corners off any triangle and they line up along a straight edge. A single parallel line explains why, and a sphere shows the line is needed.
  2. The Pythagorean theorem: a proof you can seeWhy the squares on the two short sides of a right triangle add up to the square on the long side, proved by moving four triangles inside a box.
  3. Why the area of a circle is πr²The number π is defined by the distance around a circle. Cutting the circle into sectors, and then into rings, shows why the same π gives its area.
  4. Similar triangles and why area grows as the squareScaling multiplies every length by one factor and keeps every angle. That explains shadows, maps, and why a pizza twice as wide is four times the pizza.
  5. The inscribed angle theorem and Thales' semicircleFrom every point on one arc of a circle, a chord is seen under the same angle, half the angle at the center. Two isosceles triangles explain why.
  6. Why a cone is a third of a cylinder, and a sphere's volumeA cone holds exactly one third of the cylinder around it. A cube cut into three pyramids shows why, and the same slicing idea gives the volume of a sphere.

Trigonometry

  1. The unit circle: sine and cosine as coordinatesSine and cosine start as ratios in a right triangle, which stops at 90°. Placing the triangle in a circle of radius 1 extends them to every angle.
  2. Radians: measuring an angle by the arc it cutsA radian is the angle whose arc is as long as the radius. With it, arc length, sector area and the rule sin θ ≈ θ need no conversion constants.
  3. Pythagorean identity: why sin² + cos² = 1The identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 is the Pythagorean theorem on the unit circle. Tangent and secant are lengths in the same picture and give two more.
  4. Angle addition formulas: sin(a + b) from one pictureThe sine of a sum is not the sum of the sines. Two stacked right triangles show what it is, and a rotated chord proves the formulas for every angle.
  5. Law of cosines and law of sines: solving any triangleThe law of cosines is the Pythagorean theorem corrected for angles other than 90°. With the law of sines it solves any triangle, two-answer case included.
  6. Sine waves: amplitude, frequency and phaseA sine wave is the height of a point on a turning wheel. Amplitude, frequency and phase change the wheel, and explain how two waves add, cancel or beat.

Precalculus

  1. Shifting and stretching graphs: why f(x - 2) moves rightAdding 2 outside a function lifts its graph, but subtracting 2 inside moves it right. A graph is a set of points, and tracking one point explains why.
  2. Compound interest and where e comes fromPay 100% interest in more and more instalments and a dollar grows to 2, 2.25, 2.44, 2.61, and then stops short of 2.72. That ceiling is the number e.
  3. What a logarithm is: turning multiplication into additionA logarithm answers "what exponent gives this number?". Because exponents add when powers multiply, logarithms turn every product into a sum.
  4. Complex numbers: why multiplying by i is a quarter turnNo number on the line squares to -1. Read multiplication by -1 as a half turn and the missing number appears: a quarter turn, which needs a second axis.
  5. Euler's formula: why e^(iπ) = -1You cannot multiply e by itself iπ times, so the famous equation needs a definition first. Compound interest supplies one, and it leads around a circle.
  6. Geometric series, and why 0.999... equals 1What it means to add infinitely many numbers, when a geometric series has a sum, the formula a/(1 - r) with proof, and why 0.999... is exactly 1.
  7. Roots, factors and the shape of a polynomialA cubic crosses the x axis once or three times, never exactly twice. The factor theorem, multiplicity and the leading term explain why, with proofs.

Calculus

  1. What is a derivative? Speed at a single instantA falling ball has a speed at every instant, but an instant is too short to measure a speed over. The derivative is the precise idea that resolves this.
  2. Why the derivative of x² is 2x: the power rule from picturesA growing square gains two thin strips and a tiny corner. That picture explains the 2 in 2x, and counting the same pieces in general proves the power rule.
  3. The product rule and the chain rule, seenA rectangle whose sides both change explains the product rule, and a nudge passed along a chain of number lines explains the chain rule. Both are then proved.
  4. Why the derivative of sine is cosineNudge a point around the unit circle and a tiny triangle appears, similar to the big one. Its legs are the derivatives of sine and cosine.
  5. What is a limit? The epsilon-delta definition, explainedA limit is a game. You name a tolerance, I answer with a window. From that one definition we prove the limit laws and the squeeze theorem.
  6. What is an integral? Adding up to get areaDistance from a changing speed, found by slicing time into pieces and adding. The definite integral is the limit of those sums, defined precisely and computed.
  7. The fundamental theorem of calculus, explained and provedWhy area and slope undo each other. The rate at which an area grows is the height of the curve, and that turns every integral into a subtraction.
  8. Taylor series: building functions out of polynomialsHow a calculator finds cos(0.3) with only addition and multiplication, why factorials appear, how large the error can be, and where the method breaks.
  9. Maxima and minima: where the slope is zeroTo fence the largest field or make the cheapest can, look where the derivative is zero, and also at the ends. Why that works, and when it does not.

Linear Algebra

  1. What is a vector? Arrows, lists of numbers, and adding themA vector is a list of numbers read as a movement. Adding entry by entry matches placing arrows tip to tail, and two non-parallel arrows reach the whole plane.
  2. A matrix is a transformation of spaceA 2x2 matrix moves every point of the plane, and its two columns say where the basis arrows land. Linearity is why those four numbers settle everything else.
  3. Why matrix multiplication is defined the way it isMultiplying matrices means doing one transformation after another. The rows-times-columns rule, the failure of AB = BA and associativity all follow from that.
  4. The determinant is how much area gets scaledA matrix multiplies every area in the plane by one number, ad - bc. That explains its sign, why det(AB) = det(A)det(B), and which matrices can be undone.
  5. The dot product: projection, angle and why the formula worksMultiply matching coordinates and add. The result measures how far one arrow reaches along another, which gives angles, projections and Cauchy-Schwarz.
  6. Eigenvectors: the directions a transformation leaves aloneMost arrows are knocked off their line by a matrix. The few that are only stretched are its eigenvectors, and they turn matrix powers into powers of numbers.
  7. Solving Ax = b by undoing a transformationA linear system asks which vector a matrix sends to b. That view explains when there is one solution, none or a whole line, and what the inverse matrix does.

Multivariable Calculus

  1. Surfaces and contour maps: functions of two variablesA hiking map shows a mountain on flat paper. Its contour lines are the level sets of a function of two variables, and their spacing measures steepness.
  2. The gradient: which way is uphill?On a hillside the slope depends on the direction you face. Partial derivatives give two of those slopes, and the gradient packs all of them into one arrow.
  3. Lagrange multipliers: the best point on a pathThe highest point of a trail is where the trail just touches a contour line. There the gradients of the height and of the constraint are parallel.
  4. Double integrals: volume under a surfaceVolume under a surface is a limit of sums of thin columns. Slicing turns it into two ordinary integrals, and polar coordinates handle round regions.
  5. Divergence and curl: reading a vector fieldTwo numbers at each point of a flow. Divergence is the net outflow from a tiny square per unit area, and curl is the circulation around it per unit area.
  6. Green's theorem: the inside read from the boundaryA walk around the edge of a region, adding up a vector field along the way, equals the integral of the field's curl over the inside. Here is why.

Probability

  1. What probability means: counting equally likely outcomesIs a total of 7 with two dice three times as likely as a 12, or six times? Settling it leads to sample spaces, events and the rules every probability obeys.
  2. Bayes' rule: why a positive test is usually a false alarmA 99% accurate test for a rare disease comes back positive, and the chance you are sick is about 9%. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem explain why.
  3. The birthday paradox: why 23 people is enoughIn a room of 23 people, two probably share a birthday. The exact formula, why the answer grows like a square root, and what the model assumes.
  4. Expected value and the surprising power of adding averagesThe expected value of a random quantity is its long-run average. Expected values add even for dependent quantities, which solves the hat-check problem.
  5. Binomial distribution: Pascal's triangle and a Galton boardBalls bouncing through rows of pegs pile up in the middle. Counting their paths gives Pascal's triangle and the binomial formula, with its mean and variance.
  6. The law of large numbers, and the gambler's fallacyA fair coin has no memory, yet its share of heads settles at one half. The weak law of large numbers says exactly how, and Chebyshev's inequality proves it.
  7. Monty Hall problem: why switching wins two times in threeThree doors, one car, and a host who opens a goat door. Switching wins 2/3 of the time. Three solutions, and why the answer depends on what the host knows.

Statistics

  1. Mean, median and standard deviation: what each one saysThe mean is a balance point, the median is a middle, and the standard deviation is a typical distance. Each is the best answer to a precise question.
  2. The normal distribution and the 68-95-99.7 ruleA histogram with ever narrower bins settles onto a curve whose areas are probabilities. For the bell curve, those areas give the 68-95-99.7 rule and z-scores.
  3. The central limit theorem: why bell curves are everywhereOne die is flat, but the average of 30 dice follows a bell curve. The central limit theorem says when this happens, how fast, and when it fails.
  4. Standard error: how 1,000 people speak for a countryA random sample's accuracy depends on its own size, not on the population's. We prove the square-root law for the standard error and see its limits.
  5. What a 95% confidence interval does and does not sayWe derive the interval mean ± 1.96 standard errors, test its 95% coverage by simulation, and go through the common misreadings and why each is wrong.
  6. P-values and hypothesis tests without the mysticismA coin lands heads 60 times in 100. We compute the exact p-value, define the two kinds of error, and show with Bayes' theorem what a p-value is not.
  7. Correlation and the line of best fit, derivedWe derive the least-squares line, define the correlation r and prove it lies between -1 and 1, then explain regression to the mean and why r is not causation.

Discrete Mathematics

  1. Permutations and combinations: counting without listingFive runners and three medals give 60 podiums but only 10 groups of medalists. The product rule, permutations and combinations, each one proved.
  2. Pascal's triangle and the binomial theoremRoutes across a street grid and the coefficients of (a + b)^n are the same numbers. We prove Pascal's rule, the binomial theorem and the row sums by counting.
  3. The pigeonhole principle: obvious, and surprisingly strongMore objects than boxes forces two into one box. We prove the principle and its general form, then use it on problems where finding the boxes is the hard part.
  4. Mathematical induction: infinitely many dominoesHow a finite argument proves infinitely many statements. Induction stated exactly, used on a sum and a tiling, and a famous false proof taken apart.
  5. Bridges of Königsberg: Euler paths and circuitsSeven bridges and a walk that nobody could find. Counting the edges at each vertex proves it cannot exist and settles every draw-without-lifting puzzle.
  6. Fibonacci numbers and thinking recursivelyCount the ways to climb a staircase and the Fibonacci numbers appear. Their rule, two identities, Binet's formula and the golden ratio, all proved.
  7. Clock arithmetic: modular arithmetic and why it worksArithmetic with remainders, made exact. Congruences, the divisibility tests for 9 and 11, when division is allowed, and Fermat's little theorem, with proofs.
  8. The probabilistic method: Ramsey numbers by coin flipsSix party guests always include three mutual friends or three mutual strangers. For larger parties, the best lower bounds come from coloring at random.

Logic and Proof

  1. Truth tables: and, or, not as calculationThe word "or" has two meanings in English. Truth tables fix one, and turn questions about what sentences mean into finite calculations you can check row by row.
  2. If-then statements: converse and contrapositiveWhen is "if P then Q" true? One table answers it, shows why the contrapositive says the same thing and the converse does not, and solves the four-card puzzle.
  3. Quantifiers: for all, there exists, and negationEveryone loves someone" has two meanings. Quantifiers separate them, show how to negate each, and turn the epsilon-delta definition of a limit into a game.
  4. Proof by contradiction: why √2 is not a fractionNo fraction squares to exactly 2. To prove it, assume one does and follow the assumption until it breaks. That method is proof by contradiction.
  5. Sets, Venn diagrams and inclusion-exclusionUnion, intersection and complement defined exactly, De Morgan's laws for sets, and how to count a union of overlapping sets without counting anything twice.
  6. Cantor's diagonal argument: some infinities are biggerSizes of infinite sets are compared by pairing. The integers and the fractions pair off with the counting numbers, and the real numbers provably do not.

Physics

  1. Position, velocity and acceleration: reading motion graphsSlope turns a position graph into velocity and velocity into acceleration; area goes back. The constant-acceleration equations come from one trapezoid.
  2. Newton's second law, F = ma: what force really doesForces change an object's velocity; none is needed to keep it moving. Newton's three laws, free-body diagrams, the incline and the Atwood machine in full.
  3. Projectile motion: dropped and fired bullets land togetherGravity acts only on the vertical part of a projectile's motion. From that come the parabola, the time of flight, the range, and why 45 degrees goes farthest.
  4. Conservation of energy: a roller coaster's bookkeepingWork, kinetic and potential energy, and the work-energy theorem, derived from F = ma and used to find a coaster's speed anywhere and the height a loop needs.
  5. Momentum and collisions: what survives a crashMomentum, impulse and Newton's third law, and how they fix the outcome of sticky and bouncy collisions when energy alone gives the wrong answer.
  6. Simple harmonic motion: springs and pendulumsWhy a mass on a spring takes the same time per swing however far it is pulled: Hooke's law and the shadow of circular motion, and why a pendulum nearly does.
  7. Waves and interference: how two ripples can cancelWhat a wave is, why its speed is frequency times wavelength, and how adding waves gives the quiet lines of an interference pattern and the notes of a string.
  8. Circular motion, gravity and why the Moon doesn't fallThe acceleration v²/r of circular motion, derived from the velocity triangle, and what it says about satellites, Kepler's third law and weightlessness.

Economics

  1. Supply and demand: where a price comes fromLine up buyers by what they would pay and sellers by what they would accept. The price where the two counts match is the one that leaves nobody turned away.
  2. Elasticity: when raising the price lowers revenueRevenue is a rectangle under the demand curve. Elasticity compares percentage changes in quantity and price, and decides whether a price rise grows it.
  3. Thinking at the margin: marginal cost and revenueThe right question is what one more unit adds to revenue and to cost. Why averages mislead, why sunk costs drop out, and why profit peaks where MR meets MC.
  4. Consumer surplus, and what a tax destroysMeasure what a market is worth to its buyers and sellers, then tax it. Some of the loss reaches nobody, and who bears the rest depends on slopes, not the law.
  5. Compound interest and the time value of moneyWhy a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, how to price future payments, and where the rule of 72 and the mortgage formula come from.
  6. Comparative advantage: why trade helps both sidesSomeone who is better at everything still gains by trading with someone who is worse at everything. Opportunity cost explains why, and a short proof shows when.
  7. The prisoner's dilemma and Nash equilibriumHow to read a payoff table, find best responses and Nash equilibria, mix strategies when no cell is stable, and see why repeated play can sustain cooperation.

Machine Learning

  1. What "learning" means: fitting a line to dataA model is a formula with adjustable numbers, a loss scores its misses, and training lowers the loss. All three, worked by hand on four house prices.
  2. Gradient descent: learning by walking downhillDerive the gradient descent update from the tangent line, prove exactly which learning rates converge on a parabola, and watch it train a line on real data.
  3. Classification: drawing a line between yes and noLogistic regression from the ground up: the sigmoid from log-odds, cross-entropy from maximum likelihood, the gradient derived in full, and why XOR defeats it.
  4. What a neural network computesA neuron is a linear score followed by a bend. Sums of bends build bumps, bumps build any curve, and two neurons solve XOR. All with explicit weights.
  5. Backpropagation: the chain rule, organizedRun a tiny network forward with real numbers, get every weight's slope in one backward pass, prove the delta recursion, and check it by nudging each weight.
  6. Overfitting: when a model memorizes instead of learningFit polynomials of degree 1, 3 and 9 to ten noisy points, watch training and test error part ways, and derive the bias-variance decomposition.
  7. k-means: finding groups nobody labeledScore a grouping by its sum of squared distances, lower it with Lloyd's two alternating steps, prove the algorithm must stop, and see where it goes wrong.
